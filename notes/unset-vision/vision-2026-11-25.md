UNSET — Master Vision, Product Strategy & Development Roadmap

MASTER PROJECT RECAP · NOVEMBER 25, 2026

I want to approach this as the master document for Unset: not just a list of features, but a recap of what we’ve been trying to build, why we’re building it, the architecture we’ve discussed, the decisions we’ve made, and where we should go next.

There is one important thing I want to preserve from our earlier conversations:

Unset is not supposed to be another social media platform.

It’s supposed to be something considerably bigger: a privacy-first, modular, decentralized platform that gives people control over their digital identity, their content, their communities, their data, and eventually their own little corner of the internet.

Social networking is one part of that ecosystem, not its entire purpose.

1. The vision: What exactly is Unset?

The central idea is to create an alternative to today’s centralized internet platforms.

Currently, most people depend on a handful of companies for practically everything:

* Their social identity.
* Their photographs and videos.
* Their conversations.
* Their online communities.
* Their personal websites.
* Their documents and digital files.
* Their social connections.
* Their access to information.

Those companies control the infrastructure, the rules, and frequently the data.

Unset should offer a different model.

The six fundamental principles

Principle	What it means for Unset
Privacy	Collect as little personal information as reasonably possible.
Ownership	Users should control their identities, content, and data.
Portability	Users should be able to leave without losing their digital lives.
Decentralization	People should eventually be able to host their own services and communities.
Modularity	Users should choose which applications and features they want.
Interoperability	Unset should communicate with other platforms and independent services.

The ultimate objective is to make the internet feel like it belongs to its users again.

What Unset could eventually become

Imagine someone creating an Unset account.

They receive a portable digital identity. They can create a personal homepage, publish articles, upload photographs, participate in communities, communicate privately, host their own services, and connect with people outside Unset.

Another person might use Unset almost exclusively for private communication and document collaboration.

Someone else might use it to operate a community, publish independent journalism, or host their own personal PDS.

They all use the same underlying ecosystem without necessarily using the same applications.

That’s the distinction I want to maintain throughout development.

3. Identity and user ownership

This is one of the most important parts of the entire project.

We’ve discussed using the AT Protocol ecosystem as a foundation for portable identity and user-controlled data.

The PDS concept

A Personal Data Server (PDS) allows a user to maintain their identity and associated data independently of a particular application.

The intended model:

* A user creates an Unset account.
* They receive or associate a DID.
* Their identity is associated with a PDS.
* Unset applications interact with that identity through defined interfaces.
* Users can eventually move their PDS to infrastructure they control.

The objective is to avoid making an Unset account synonymous with permanent dependence on Unset infrastructure.

Our PDS hosting possibilities

We’ve discussed several levels of hosting:

* Default hosting: Unset provides the infrastructure.
* Self-hosted PDS: Technically capable users operate their own.
* Dedicated hosting: Users or organizations receive isolated infrastructure.
* Mini PDS: Small, inexpensive hardware for families or individuals.

We recently explored the possibility of a very lightweight Rust PDS implementation, potentially running on approximately 500 MB of RAM, and inexpensive hardware such as Raspberry Pi-class devices.

We also discussed family PDS deployments, with an emphasis on minimizing hardware and operating costs.

The dedicated-container idea

A particularly interesting direction from our recent conversations is allowing users to run isolated services that Unset cannot inspect.

The idea is:

* Give a user their own dedicated environment.
* Encrypt their data and storage.
* Restrict operator access.
* Let their service communicate with the wider Unset ecosystem through controlled interfaces.
* Preserve interoperability while separating private data from the main platform.

This is an important long-term objective, but it needs careful technical design. Encryption at rest alone does not prevent an administrator from reading data while a service is actively processing it. Stronger isolation, key ownership, and potentially confidential-computing techniques would need to be considered.

The principle: users should not have to trust Unset with everything simply because they use Unset.

6. Communication: Unset Chat

Communication is another major pillar.

We’ve discussed using Matrix rather than reinventing an entire messaging protocol.

What we want

* Private one-to-one messaging.
* Private group conversations.
* Community chat rooms.
* Voice calls.
* Video calls.
* Discord-like voice channels.
* Potentially larger community spaces.
* Integration with Unset identity.

Our Matrix strategy

We’ve discussed two possibilities:

* Build our own Matrix client and interface.
* Offer Element as an additional client or integration later.

My preference from our discussions has been to have Unset’s own experience rather than simply wrapping another application.

Matrix should provide the underlying communication infrastructure.

Privacy requirements

This is particularly important.

We want:

* End-to-end encryption where supported.
* Minimal server-side logging.
* No unnecessary access to message contents.
* User-controlled encryption keys.
* Clear distinctions between public rooms and private conversations.
* Privacy-conscious integrations and bridges.

We also discussed hosting bridges, potentially allowing users to connect external services without giving Unset operators access to plaintext messages.

One important distinction: Matrix federation and end-to-end encryption are separate concerns. Federation allows servers to communicate; encryption determines who can read message contents.

We should design both deliberately.

7. Communities, forums and federation

This is where Unset could become much more than a social network.

We’ve discussed allowing communities to exist independently rather than forcing everyone into communities hosted by Unset.

The community concept

A community could have:

* Its own administrators.
* Its own moderation policies.
* Its own members.
* Its own content.
* Its own storage.
* Its own hosting infrastructure.
* Its own federation settings.

We also discussed a forum or imageboard-like experience inspired by traditional internet communities.

Potential directions include:

* Thread-based discussions.
* Anonymous or pseudonymous posting.
* Optional verified AT Protocol identity.
* ActivityPub interoperability.
* Independent community hosting.
* User-hosted PDS identities.

The idea is to support both identified and anonymous participation.

External community discovery

Another important idea was having Unset discover and display content from independently hosted communities.

Think of Unset as a discovery and participation layer across a network of independent destinations.

A community could exist outside Unset but still be discoverable through it.

Moderation and responsibility

We had several conversations about the legal and operational implications of this model.

You don’t want Unset to become an organization that constantly polices every conversation.

At the same time, you don’t want anonymity and decentralization to become excuses for ignoring abuse or illegal material.

Our direction has been:

* Clear separation between platform infrastructure and independently operated communities.
* Community-level moderation.
* Delegated administration.
* Reporting mechanisms.
* Transparent rules.
* Appropriate handling of unlawful content.
* Clear responsibility boundaries.
* Minimal logging consistent with safety and legal requirements.

An important qualification: decentralizing hosting does not automatically eliminate Unset’s legal responsibilities. Indexing, recommending, storing, moderating, and operating independent services can create different responsibilities depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances.

This is an area where we should obtain proper legal advice before launching the federation features.

9. The Privacy Center

This is one of the most distinctive ideas we’ve discussed.

Rather than simply advertising Unset as privacy-friendly, we want to help users understand their actual privacy exposure.

The Privacy Center would be an educational and practical privacy-audit tool.

Two main directions

A. What can others see about me?

A user could examine their digital exposure:

* Browser fingerprinting.
* Public profile information.
* Public activity.
* Email breach exposure.
* Data broker information.
* Location exposure, where measurable.
* External account privacy settings.
* Publicly accessible information.

B. What can I do to improve my privacy?

The system could suggest:

* Browser alternatives.
* Privacy-respecting search engines.
* Safer document services.
* Better account settings.
* Reduced tracking.
* Improved profile privacy.
* Better storage practices.

We mentioned tools and services such as CryptPad as examples of privacy-conscious alternatives.

The privacy report

We also discussed generating a downloadable PDF report.

The proposed experience:

* The user initiates an audit.
* Unset collects only the information needed for the checks.
* The results are assembled into a report.
* The user downloads the report.
* The report can be password-encrypted.
* The password is shown once.
* Unset avoids retaining the report or unnecessary audit information.

We want as much of this as possible to be self-hosted.

Some external APIs might still be useful, but we should evaluate what data they receive and whether the same functionality can be provided locally.

10. The Interest Profile and transparent recommendations

This was another important addition to our discussions.

We talked about an optional Interest Profile that would allow users to see what Unset thinks they are interested in.

The idea is to avoid an opaque recommendation algorithm.

For example, Unset might infer that someone is interested in:

* Technology.
* Privacy.
* Independent journalism.
* Photography.
* Open-source software.

But users should be able to inspect exactly why those interests appear.

User controls

We established several important requirements:

* See inferred interest labels.
* See the signals behind each label.
* Add interests manually.
* Remove individual interests.
* Dismiss recommendations.
* Reset the profile.
* Disable inferred-interest personalization completely.

Most importantly, we established a privacy boundary.

Only permitted public activity and explicitly selected interests may be used.

Examples of potentially permitted signals:

* Public follows.
* Public community memberships.
* Public posts and tags.
* Explicitly selected interests.

The following must never be used for interest inference:

* Private Matrix conversations.
* Private community memberships.
* Drafts.
* Bookmarks.

This is a very important product principle: personalization should be transparent, controllable, and limited to information users have agreed can be used.

12. Technical architecture: How we’re building Unset

This is where we’ve spent quite a bit of time recently.

Your stated priority is to establish a strong technical foundation, even if that means delaying the public launch.

You are building primarily with AI assistance, and you want to direct the architecture rather than blindly accept whatever code an AI produces.

I think that is an important distinction.

The architecture needs to be understandable, maintainable, modular, testable, and secure enough that another developer could eventually take over.

12.1 Architectural philosophy

We’ve established the direction of a modular monolith first, with independently deployable services where justified later.

We don’t want to start with dozens of microservices.

The application should be divided into clear boundaries:

UI / Applications

SvelteKit · Profiles · Social · Settings · Chat

Interfaces / Contracts

APIs · Validation · Module boundaries · Adapters

Domain / Business Logic

Identity · Permissions · Profiles · Content · Communities

Infrastructure

Database · Storage · External providers · Workers

Deployment / Operations

Docker · CI/CD · Backups · Monitoring · Security

The principle is that the UI should not directly depend on infrastructure implementation details.

For example, if we replace Garage with another S3-compatible storage provider, the profile editor shouldn’t need to know.

Likewise, if we replace a search provider, the rest of the application should continue working through the same interface.

PostgreSQL and Supabase

We recently discussed Supabase and how it relates to PostgreSQL.

The distinction we established is that PostgreSQL is the database itself, whereas Supabase provides a collection of additional services and tooling around it.

We want to understand which services we actually need rather than blindly adopting the entire Supabase ecosystem.

Drizzle remains part of the proposed architecture for application-level database access.

Redis / Upstash

We also discussed Redis and Upstash.

The important architectural distinction is that Redis is an infrastructure component that can provide caching, rate limiting, ephemeral state, and queue-related capabilities.

Upstash is a managed service option, not a requirement to use Redis as a hosted dependency.

We should retain the ability to self-host Redis-compatible infrastructure.

OpenFGA versus Cerbos

We spent time comparing these authorization approaches.

Our direction was to favor OpenFGA for Unset’s relationship-based authorization requirements.

The reasoning was that Unset has complex relationships:

* Users.
* Organizations.
* Communities.
* Content.
* Administrators.
* Moderators.
* Shared resources.
* Delegated permissions.

We want permissions to be expressed through relationships rather than scattering authorization decisions throughout the application.

Cerbos was also considered, particularly for policy-based authorization, but OpenFGA was the preferred fit in our discussions.

We should still maintain a clear separation between authentication and authorization.

Authentication answers who someone is.

Authorization answers what they are allowed to do.

12.3 Storage architecture

We’ve discussed using S3-compatible object storage.

Garage is the intended self-hosted solution, particularly for development and staging.

The general design:

* PostgreSQL stores metadata.
* Object storage stores files.
* The application controls authorization.
* Users can eventually select storage destinations.
* User-owned data can be separated into logical storage pools.

We also discussed having PDS-associated storage for profile pictures, attachments, and other user-owned files.

File upload implementation

Our more recent discussion established a direction involving Uppy.

The intended upload flow:

* The frontend initiates an upload.
* Elysia validates authorization.
* The application creates or authorizes the storage upload.
* Uppy handles multipart/resumable upload.
* The file goes directly to object storage.
* PostgreSQL records metadata.

This avoids unnecessarily routing large files through the main application server.

We also discussed creating a storage-provider integration interface while deferring Nango rather than adding an integration platform prematurely.

12.4 Deployment architecture

Your infrastructure discussions have centered around:

* Proxmox as the virtualization platform.
* Docker for application deployment.
* Garage for storage.
* A home server for self-hosted services.
* Pangolin on a public VPS to protect the home IP and provide connectivity.

The idea is to use a public VPS as the entry point while keeping much of the actual infrastructure at home.

We also discussed DNS and security providers, including deSEC, Cloudflare, and privacy-conscious CDN/DDoS protection options such as Bunny.

These choices still need to be validated against the final threat model and deployment design.

Version channels

In our recent deployment discussion, you selected:

Stable version channel

Rather than automatically deploying latest or beta, the intention is to favor predictable, tested deployments.

12.5 Security and engineering standards

This is arguably the most important technical aspect of Unset.

You already have security standards and checklists in the repository, and you want the security architecture to be part of development rather than something added afterward.

Some of the specific areas we’ve discussed:

* Strict module boundaries.
* Identity and session security.
* Secure cookies.
* Rate limiting.
* Trusted proxy handling.
* Authorization enforcement.
* Minimal logging.
* Input validation.
* Secure file uploads.
* Data isolation.
* Backup and recovery.
* Automated testing.
* Dependency management.
* Security reviews.

We also discussed your existing session design:

* __Host-sid cookie.
* Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite=Lax.
* Path /.
* 32 bytes of cryptographically random session material.
* SHA-256 hash stored in the database rather than the raw session token.

And an important code-review item:

Email verification must not cause a persistent state mutation through a GET request.

We specifically discussed investigating setEmailConfirmed(...) in the GET /verify-email flow and moving the mutation to an appropriate protected POST or background process, while preserving authentication, CSRF protections, and idempotence requirements.

This is an example of the level of engineering scrutiny we want throughout Unset.

16. What I would actually prioritize over the next 12 months

The eight phases describe the overall vision. They shouldn’t be mistaken for a promise to complete everything in one year.

Given your preference for a strong foundation over rushing a launch, I would use the following more realistic planning framework.

Period	Main objective
Months 1–3	Complete and stabilize the core
Months 4–5	Identity, profiles and personal spaces
Months 6–7	Storage, publishing and basic social
Months 8–9	Feeds, discovery and search
Months 10–11	Matrix integration and initial communities
Month 12	Security review, controlled pilot and stabilization

These are planning windows, not fixed commitments. If the core requires longer, it should take longer.

I would deliberately defer the marketplace, sophisticated recommendation systems, complex video infrastructure, and advanced monetization.

A good first-year achievement would be a relatively small but exceptionally solid Unset ecosystem.

17. What we should NOT do

I think this deserves its own section because it will help protect the project from architectural complexity.

There are several temptations we should resist.

Avoid	Why
Starting with microservices everywhere	Unnecessary operational complexity
Building our own messaging protocol	Matrix already solves much of this problem
Building our own identity protocol	AT Protocol provides an existing ecosystem
Allowing arbitrary profile JavaScript	Serious security risks
Implementing every federation protocol immediately	Too much complexity before the core is proven
Building a marketplace too early	No stable module ecosystem yet
Collecting unnecessary analytics	Conflicts with privacy principles
Building complicated recommendation algorithms	Not necessary for initial product viability
Prematurely optimizing everything	We need measurements first
Making Unset dependent on one storage provider	Conflicts with portability

I would also establish one rule for AI-assisted development:

No new major feature should be implemented without an architectural brief, defined interfaces, security requirements, and tests.

This is especially important when multiple AI agents are working on different parts of the repository.

18. The major architectural and product questions still to resolve

These are not necessarily problems. They are decisions that should be made deliberately as the project matures.

Question	Why it matters

How do users migrate their identity?	Fundamental to ownership
Which data belongs in the PDS versus application databases?	Determines data boundaries
How will third-party modules be sandboxed?	Essential for security

We should know exactly which data belongs to:

* The user’s PDS.
* Matrix.
* Object storage.
* An independently operated community.
* A third-party module.

That decision will influence practically every future feature.

The point isn’t to compete with every one of these projects by reinventing their technologies.

Unset should be the place where people can access and manage their digital lives without being locked into one company.

That is a much more ambitious project than simply making another social network.

20. My recommended definition of Unset

If we had to explain the entire project to a developer, potential collaborator, or future investor in one statement, I would use something like this:

UNSET

Your identity. Your data. Your space on the internet.

Unset is a privacy-first, modular and decentralized digital ecosystem designed to give individuals control over their online identity, content, communications, communities and infrastructure.

Through portable identity, user-controlled storage, interoperable applications and independently operated services, Unset aims to provide an alternative to centralized digital platforms.

Rather than forcing users into a single application or ecosystem, Unset provides the foundations upon which individuals, communities and developers can build their own digital experiences.

My final thoughts

I think the most important thing we’ve established over all these conversations is that Unset needs to be built in layers.

You have a lot of ambitious ideas, but they actually fit together under one coherent philosophy.

The first layer is trust: identity, security, permissions, storage, and user control.

The second layer is usefulness: profiles, publishing, social networking, messaging, and communities.

The third layer is independence: self-hosting, federation, portable infrastructure, and third-party applications.

The fourth layer is sustainability: hosting, optional paid services, modules, and an ecosystem that can support itself without selling user privacy.

Don’t sacrifice the first layer to rush the other three.

I would rather see Unset launch later with a genuinely solid technical foundation than launch early and discover that its architecture cannot support the very things that make the project different.

And one final point: I don’t think we should measure Unset’s success by how many features it has compared with Bluesky, Discord, Reddit, or Nextcloud.

We should measure it by how much control a user has over their digital life after joining Unset.

That’s the product we’re trying to build.
